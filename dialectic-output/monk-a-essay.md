# The Ineffable Is Just the Not-Yet-Measured

There is a recurring moment in the history of science. Someone points to a phenomenon — the quality of a mind, the chemistry between two people, the character of a wine — and declares it beyond the reach of measurement. The phenomenon is too holistic, too relational, too suffused with subjective meaning to survive decomposition into numbers. And then, slowly and sometimes painfully, measurement science catches up. Not by reducing the phenomenon to something lesser, but by developing instruments sophisticated enough to capture what matters about it.

We are at exactly this moment with AI model evaluation. The user who reports that Claude "far outstrips others in intent comprehension and real-world usefulness" despite not leading on benchmarks has made an accurate observation. Current benchmarks are broken. MMLU is contaminated — a multi-method audit found 13.8% contamination overall, spiking to 66.7% in Philosophy. Frontier models saturate past 90% on standard tests. A model's accuracy can swing 25 percentage points depending on prompt format alone. These instruments are measuring the wrong things, measuring them badly, and being gamed by model providers who optimize for the scorecard rather than the capability. On all of this, I agree completely.

Where I part company is the further inference: that the quality which actually matters — the "je ne sais quoi" of a model being genuinely useful — is fundamentally unmeasurable. That it is ontologically irreducible, resistant in principle to systematic evaluation. This is not a radical insight. It is a pre-scientific intuition, and the history of measurement science shows us exactly how such intuitions dissolve.

## The Psychometric Precedent

In 1904, Charles Spearman noticed that children who performed well on one cognitive test tended to perform well on others and proposed a general factor of intelligence, *g*. The response was immediate and fierce. Intelligence, critics argued, was too multifaceted, too embedded in cultural context, too bound up with the whole person to be captured by any test. The debate was not trivial — it involved real philosophical substance about reductionism, cultural bias, and the nature of mind.

And then psychometrics matured. Not by collapsing intelligence into a single number, but by developing increasingly sophisticated multidimensional models. The Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory now maps cognitive abilities across three strata of specificity. Factor analysis revealed structure where intuition saw only an undifferentiated whole. The measurement did not destroy the complexity; it illuminated it.

Personality followed the same arc. In 1936, Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert catalogued 4,500 English words describing personality differences — a seemingly hopeless thicket of holistic human variation. Through decades of factor-analytic work, from Cattell's 16 factors to Eysenck's dimensions to the eventual convergence on the Big Five, personality psychology decomposed what felt irreducibly complex into five broad, measurable dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Each measured on a continuous scale, each with subfacets, each validated across cultures and languages. The Big Five did not strip personality of its richness. It gave us a map of that richness — one that predicts job performance, relationship satisfaction, and mental health outcomes with meaningful accuracy.

The person who insisted in 1930 that personality was too holistic for measurement was not wrong about personality's complexity. They were wrong about measurement's ceiling.

## Relational Properties Are Measurable

The strongest version of the opposing argument holds that model usefulness is not a property of the model at all — it is a relational property that exists in the interaction between user and system. Drawing on Buber's distinction between I-Thou encounter and I-It objectification, the claim is that benchmarks necessarily treat models as objects, while the quality that matters emerges only in genuine encounter. You cannot measure the thing because the thing does not exist outside the relationship.

I take this seriously. And I respond: relational properties are measured successfully across multiple scientific disciplines, every day.

The most precise analogue is the therapeutic alliance in psychotherapy — the quality of the working relationship between therapist and client. This is as relational, holistic, and experiential a property as one could ask for. It exists between two people. It involves trust, mutual understanding, shared goals, and emotional attunement. Clinicians long insisted it was ineffable, a matter of feel and intuition.

In 1989, Adam Horvath and Leslie Greenberg published the Working Alliance Inventory. Built on Edward Bordin's theoretical framework, it operationalized the alliance into three measurable dimensions: agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and the quality of the interpersonal bond. The WAI achieves Cronbach's alpha coefficients exceeding 0.90. It has been translated into more than 18 languages. A meta-analysis found it used in 80 of 201 studies linking psychotherapy outcomes to the working relationship. Most critically, WAI scores measured early in treatment reliably predict therapeutic outcomes. The ineffable was made effable. The relational was made measurable. And the measurement made clinical practice better, not worse — therapists who monitor alliance scores achieve better outcomes than those who rely on gut feeling alone.

User experience research accomplished the same feat. The System Usability Scale, the User Experience Questionnaire, the UMUX — all measure properties that emerge in the interaction between human and interface, not in the interface alone. Sports analytics now quantifies team chemistry, the quintessential "more than the sum of its parts" phenomenon. In every case, the unit of analysis shifted from the object to the interaction, and measurement followed.

The fact that model usefulness is relational does not make it unmeasurable. It means the unit of analysis is the interaction, not the model. This is a methodological adjustment, not an ontological barrier.

## The Measurement Science Is Already Beginning

The claim that AI evaluation cannot capture what matters is particularly untenable given what is happening right now in the field.

VibeCheck, published at ICLR 2025 by Lisa Dunlap and colleagues, directly operationalizes the "vibes" that users report as distinguishing models. It automatically discovers qualitative traits — tone, formatting preferences, explanatory style, emotional register — and measures them quantitatively. These discovered vibes predict model identity with 80% accuracy and human preference with 61% accuracy. That 61% number is not ceiling; it is the first reading from a new instrument class. The WAI's early validity coefficients were modest too before decades of refinement.

Serapio-García, Safdari and colleagues published in *Nature Machine Intelligence* in late 2025 a psychometric framework for administering and validating personality assessments on LLMs. They demonstrated that personality measurements in model outputs can be reliable and valid, that evidence of reliability is stronger for larger and instruction-tuned models, and that personality can be shaped along specific dimensions. Anthropic's work on persona vectors shows that model character traits can be identified, monitored, and controlled at the representation level — measured not through behavioral proxies but through direct inspection of the model's internal geometry.

Bean and Rocher's "Measuring What Matters" framework, presented at NeurIPS 2025 with 40 contributing researchers, provides the theoretical scaffolding. After reviewing 445 benchmarks, they found that 78% fail to define their target phenomenon clearly, only 16% include statistical testing, and nearly half use contested definitions. Their diagnosis is not that measurement is impossible but that current measurement is sloppy. Their eight recommendations — including construct validity justification, statistical testing requirements, and clear phenomenon definition — are a roadmap for doing it properly. This is the transition from phrenology to psychometrics, happening in real time.

## The Goodhart Problem Requires Better Measurement, Not No Measurement

The most formidable objection is Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The argument is that making "attunement" or "intent comprehension" measurable will inevitably cause model providers to optimize the proxy, degrading the underlying quality. RLHF reward hacking is cited as structural proof that proxy optimization is not a bug to be fixed but a mathematical theorem about the impossibility of faithful proxies.

I will not dismiss this with "we'll be more careful." The Goodhart problem is real and has formal teeth — Manheim and Garrabrant's taxonomy identifies regressive, extremal, causal, and adversarial failure modes, and there are formal proofs that for any environment and true reward function, no non-trivial unhackable proxy exists.

But consider what this argument actually entails. If Goodhart's Law is an argument against measuring model quality, it is equally an argument against measuring anything that matters — blood pressure, educational achievement, therapeutic progress, software quality, economic health. Every one of these domains faces the Goodhart problem. Every one of them has addressed it not by abandoning measurement but by developing measurement ecosystems: multiple orthogonal metrics, construct validity requirements, external auditing, qualitative complements to quantitative scores, and deliberate decoupling of measurement from optimization targets.

Medicine does not abandon blood pressure measurement because of white-coat hypertension. It adds ambulatory monitoring, contextual baselines, and multiple readings. Software engineering does not abandon quality metrics because lines-of-code is gameable. It develops DORA's four-metric framework where optimizing one at the expense of others becomes visible. The Thomas and Uminsky solution — slates of metrics, qualitative accounts, stakeholder involvement — is a measurement improvement, not a retreat from measurement.

For AI evaluation specifically, the response is multi-layered: use diverse evaluation paradigms simultaneously (arena-style preference, task completion, persona analysis, interaction quality assessment), maintain held-out evaluation sets unknown to model providers, employ construct validity auditing as Bean and Rocher recommend, and build evaluation infrastructure independent of the entities being evaluated (as METR does for frontier model assessment, accepting no compensation to maintain independence). Goodhart's Law is an argument for measurement engineering. It is not an argument for measurement nihilism.

## The Cost of the Alternative

Strip away systematic evaluation, and what remains? No reproducibility. No safety frameworks. No rational basis for model comparison. No accountability. The user's preference for Claude becomes an unfalsifiable vibe — indistinguishable, in principle, from familiarity bias, mere-exposure effect, or brand loyalty.

If Claude genuinely excels at intent comprehension, that should be demonstrable. If it is not demonstrable, we must ask: how do we know? Intuition is demonstrably unreliable — studies find cognitive biases affecting judgment in 36.5% to 77% of medical case scenarios, and clinicians are no less expert in their domain than AI users are in theirs. The wine parallel is instructive: expert judges vary by plus or minus four points when rating the same wine on different occasions, and algorithms predicting wine quality from physicochemical variables achieve 91% accuracy. Sixty-eight percent of sommeliers dismiss algorithmic assessment as "soulless." The algorithms are still more reliable.

The "je ne sais quoi" feeling is real as phenomenology. The user genuinely experiences something when working with a model that attends well to intent. But that experience is not evidence of irreducibility. It is evidence that the user's System 1 — fast, holistic, pattern-recognizing — has access to information that their System 2 has not yet decomposed into articulable criteria. This is precisely Kahneman's framework: expert intuition that feels ineffable but, under analysis, resolves into a set of cues processed below conscious awareness. The sommelier who "just knows" a great Burgundy is responding to acidity, tannin structure, volatile compounds, and a hundred other analyzable dimensions. The user who "just knows" Claude is better is responding to response latency patterns, hedging frequencies, instruction-following precision, tone calibration, and dozens of other analyzable interaction features.

The history of science is the history of making the ineffable effable. Vitalism said life could not be reduced to chemistry. It could. Behaviorism said mental states could not be scientifically studied. They could. The conviction that model quality has an irreducible core beyond measurement's reach is the same intuition, applied to a new domain. It will meet the same fate.

## The Work Ahead

I am not claiming that current evaluation captures what matters. It manifestly does not. I am claiming that the project of systematic evaluation — the commitment to developing instruments that can measure model quality in its full multidimensional, relational, contextual richness — is sound, necessary, and already producing early results. The gap between what we can measure today and what we need to measure is an engineering challenge, not a metaphysical limit.

The user deserves better than "trust your vibes." They deserve evaluation instruments as sophisticated as their experience — tools that can confirm or disconfirm their intuition that Claude excels at intent comprehension, that can identify exactly which dimensions of interaction quality drive that experience, and that can hold model providers accountable to the qualities that actually matter. Building those instruments is difficult. It will take years. It will require the same kind of iterative refinement that took psychometrics from Spearman's *g* to the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model, that took therapeutic alliance from clinical intuition to the WAI.

But the trajectory is clear. Measurement science has never encountered a phenomenon that mattered to humans and permanently failed to measure it. The "je ne sais quoi" of model quality will not be the first.
