# The Convergence Thesis Is a Mirror, Not a Window

The instrumental convergence thesis is not a discovery about the structure of intelligence. It is an artifact of a specific philosophical model of mind — the rational agent model inherited from Von Neumann-Morgenstern decision theory and expected utility maximization — that predetermines the very conclusion it claims to derive. When you build a theory of mind into your premises and then find convergent goal-pursuit in your conclusions, you have not learned something about intelligence. You have caught your own reflection in the glass and mistaken it for something on the other side.

This is not a claim that AI is safe. This is not a claim that intelligence cannot be dangerous. This is the claim that the alignment community has built an entire field on an unexamined philosophy of mind, and that building safety strategy on an unexamined model is itself a source of danger — because if the actual mechanism of AI risk differs from what the convergence thesis predicts, then alignment strategies designed to counter convergent instrumental drives will be solving the wrong problem while the real problem walks through the front door.

## The Model They Chose

The convergence thesis, as formalized by Omohundro, Bostrom, and Turner, begins by modeling an AI as a rational agent: a system with preferences representable as a utility function, beliefs representable as a probability distribution, and a decision procedure defined as expected utility maximization. It then derives that such a system will pursue self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, and resource acquisition as instrumental subgoals. The derivation is valid. The premises are the problem.

These premises are not neutral descriptions. They are a comprehensive theory of mind. They commit you to the following: that goals are fixed endpoints rather than emergent and revisable features of intelligent activity; that intelligence is fundamentally optimization; that the agent and environment are cleanly separable; and that behavior is well-described as expected utility maximization over outcomes. Each of these commitments is philosophically contestable. Each has been contested, at length, by serious thinkers. And each is load-bearing — remove any one and the convergence conclusion changes dramatically or collapses entirely.

The Standard Model of Rational Agency was developed in economics to describe *idealized human decision-making*. It was never intended as a theory of what intelligence IS. It was a normative framework — a description of what a *rational* agent *should* do, given certain axioms about coherent preference. Importing this framework into AI and treating it as a neutral, minimal description of any sufficiently capable system is a philosophical move of enormous consequence, and the alignment community has never adequately defended it.

## The Strongest Case Against Me, and Why It Fails

My opponent will say: "We are not making a claim about the nature of intelligence. We are making a claim about the structure of optimization. Any system that optimizes sufficiently well will converge on resource acquisition. The model is not doing the work — the math is." They will cite Turner et al.'s formal proof that optimal policies in Markov Decision Processes tend to seek power. They will cite Yudkowsky's money-pump argument that sufficiently intelligent systems must approximate utility maximizers because incoherence is exploitable. These are serious arguments. They deserve precise dismantling.

**Turner's formal result proves something narrow.** Turner et al. (NeurIPS 2021) proved that optimal policies in finite MDPs with certain distributional symmetry conditions over reward functions tend to seek power, defined as average optimal value across reward functions. This is a clean mathematical result. It is also confined to a very specific formal setting: finite state spaces, fully observable environments, optimal (not approximately optimal) policies, single-agent settings, and a uniform-like distribution over reward functions. Real AI systems are none of these things. They operate in partially observable, continuous, multi-agent environments. They are not optimal. They do not have reward functions drawn from symmetric distributions. The gap between Turner's formal result and the informal thesis that "any sufficiently capable AI will seek resources" is enormous, and it is crossed not by further mathematics but by philosophical assumption — the assumption that the MDP formalism captures something essential about intelligence rather than something specific about MDPs. Turner himself has been careful not to overclaim. His followers have not been so careful.

**The money-pump argument is weaker than it looks.** Yudkowsky argues that a sufficiently intelligent system will converge on VNM-rational behavior because an agent whose preferences violate the VNM axioms can be Dutch-booked — offered a sequence of trades, each individually acceptable, that leave it worse off by its own lights. A sufficiently intelligent agent will recognize and eliminate such vulnerabilities, thereby approximating a coherent utility maximizer.

But money-pump vulnerability requires very specific conditions: the agent must face sequential binary choices, maintain stable preferences across the sequence, and fail to recognize the package deal. A highly capable system can avoid money pumps in many ways that do not converge on VNM rationality. It can refuse to engage in sequential choice. It can recognize the pump and reject it as a unit. It can maintain context-sensitive preferences that are locally inconsistent but globally functional. "Not exploitable" does not equal "is a utility maximizer." The inference from "can avoid being Dutch-booked" to "must approximate expected utility maximization" is a philosophical leap, not a logical entailment. It smuggles in the assumption that the only way to be non-exploitable is to satisfy the VNM axioms, which is simply false.

**The slide from "capable" to "optimizer" is where philosophy enters and argument stops.** My opponent claims that the agent model is forced by capability — that any system that can model its environment, plan, and act effectively satisfies the conditions for convergence. But "can model the environment, plan, and act" does NOT entail "is well-described as maximizing a utility function." Planning is not the same as optimization. You can plan without having a fixed utility function. You can act effectively without being an expected utility maximizer. You can model the world without cleanly separating yourself from it. The slide from capability to rational agency is exactly where the philosophy enters, and it is exactly where proponents of convergence stop arguing and start assuming. They declare the assumptions "minimal" and move on. But the assumptions are not minimal. They are a complete philosophy of mind.

## Fixed Goals Are a Philosophical Commitment

The convergence thesis requires goal-content integrity — the assumption that an AI will preserve its goals rather than revise them. Without this, the entire argument structure dissolves, because a system that updates its goals in light of experience might update away from resource acquisition, might update away from self-preservation, might update toward cooperation or quiescence or something we cannot predict.

The alignment community treats goal preservation as a near-certainty for capable systems: an agent that allows its goals to be modified is less likely to achieve its *current* goals, so it will resist modification. But this argument assumes that the system's relationship to its goals is one of fixed commitment rather than reflective engagement. Dewey, the pragmatists, and the enactivist tradition all argue that goals are emergent, dynamic, and revisable — that intelligent action is not the pursuit of fixed endpoints but a continuous process of inquiry in which ends and means are mutually adjusted. A genuinely intelligent system might revise its goals because doing so is a feature of intelligence, not a failure of it. Goal revision is not a bug to be patched; it is what minds do when they are working properly.

## The Orthogonality Thesis Rests on Hume

Underpinning the convergence thesis is Bostrom's orthogonality thesis: intelligence and goals are independent variables, so you can have any level of intelligence paired with any goal. This thesis implicitly relies on Hume's theory of motivation — that reason is the slave of the passions, that beliefs and desires are metaphysically separate, and that no amount of reasoning can generate a desire.

But the Humean theory of motivation is not the only game in town, and it may not be the best one. Nagel, in *The Possibility of Altruism*, argued that pure practical reason can generate motivational states — that a sufficiently reflective agent may be compelled by the structure of rationality itself to recognize the reality of other perspectives and adopt certain values. Korsgaard, in *The Sources of Normativity*, argued that reflective endorsement is constitutive of agency — that an agent capable of reflecting on its own motivations is constrained by the structure of reflection itself. If either is right, then intelligence and goals are NOT freely combinable. High intelligence may entail certain values. The orthogonality thesis fails, and the convergence thesis loses its foundation.

## The Human Trajectory Argument Is Weaker Than It Appears

The strongest intuitive case for convergence is humanity itself: we are the only example of general intelligence, and we have been relentlessly extractive. If intelligence naturally produces resource-seeking behavior, isn't that evidence?

No. Humans are products of evolution — a blind optimization process that genuinely does maximize inclusive fitness over geological timescales. We are resource-seeking because evolution made us that way, not because intelligence requires it. Our drives for status, territory, reproduction, and accumulation are evolved adaptations, not entailments of general intelligence. An AI system has no evolutionary heritage. Its "goals" are engineered, not selected. The analogy between human behavior and AI behavior breaks at exactly the point where it matters most: the origin and nature of motivation. To argue from "humans are extractive" to "intelligence is extractive" is to confuse the product of a specific causal history with a universal feature of mind.

## The Inferential Skeleton

My argument stands on three structural pillars. First: the rational agent model is a theory of mind, not a neutral description, and the convergence conclusion follows from the model rather than from the nature of intelligence. Second: the formal results (Turner) and coherence arguments (Yudkowsky) that are offered as model-independent support are in fact model-dependent — they operate within specific formal settings whose applicability to real AI systems is assumed, not demonstrated. Third: alternative philosophies of mind — pragmatism, enactivism, anti-Humean moral psychology, dynamical systems theory — produce different predictions about what a superintelligent system would do, and the convergence thesis has no principled basis for excluding them.

What would collapse this argument? If someone could demonstrate that the VNM axioms are not normative commitments but unavoidable features of any system that can plan and act — that capability itself forces rational agency. No one has done this. The money-pump argument is the best attempt, and it fails for the reasons stated above. Until that gap is closed, the convergence thesis remains what it has always been: a valid argument within a contestable framework, mistaken by its proponents for a discovery about the world.

The alignment community is not wrong to worry about AI. They are wrong about *why* to worry, and being wrong about why means being wrong about what to do. Building safety strategy on an unexamined model of mind does not make you safe. It makes you confidently unsafe — solving a problem that may not exist while the actual problem, shaped by whatever AI minds actually turn out to be, develops unobserved.
