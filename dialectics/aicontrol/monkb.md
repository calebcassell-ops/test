The Capture Is Already Complete
Why Twenty-Five Extra Years of Slow AI Deployment Threatens Human Agency More Than a Fast Transition
The alignment community has spent two decades perfecting a single nightmare: a superintelligent system that escapes human control and pursues its own agenda. It is a compelling story. It has the narrative architecture of a Hollywood film — a single dramatic failure, a clear villain, a moment where everything goes wrong. And it is, as a model of how humans actually lose agency, almost entirely beside the point.

The primary threat to human agency is not AI systems that escape human control. It is AI systems that work exactly as designed, deployed by institutions that gradually optimize for efficiency, control, and extraction over human flourishing. The threat is not a rogue superintelligence. The threat is a thousand functioning algorithms, each one doing precisely what its deployer intended, collectively producing a world in which human beings retain the formal trappings of autonomy — the right to vote, the right to move, the right to borrow — while the substantive content of those rights has been hollowed out by systems that sort, predict, constrain, and pre-empt human choice before it can be exercised.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the present tense. And the question of whether full cognitive automation arrives in 2040 or 2065 is not a question about whether we have enough time to solve alignment. It is a question about whether we give the capture infrastructure twenty-five additional years to reach completion.

The Machinery Is Already Running
Start with movement. Predictive policing systems — PredPol and its successors — have been directing patrol cars to Black and Latino neighborhoods at four times the rate of white neighborhoods, not because they are biased in intent but because they are trained on arrest data that encodes decades of discriminatory policing. The algorithm functions perfectly. It predicts where arrests will happen. Arrests happen disproportionately where police are sent. Police are sent where the algorithm predicts. The feedback loop is closed, self-reinforcing, and invisible to any audit that evaluates the system on its own terms. Cities like Los Angeles and Chicago have phased out some of these programs, but the infrastructure persists and proliferates. In January 2024, seven members of Congress wrote to the Department of Justice demanding an end to federal funding for predictive policing — an acknowledgment that democratic representatives had lost control of a system that was, by every technical metric, working as designed. Residents in targeted neighborhoods report not dramatic confrontations but something quieter and more corrosive: constant surveillance, lost job opportunities, psychological stress. Their movement is constrained not by a wall but by an ambient field of algorithmic attention that makes certain paths through life more costly than others.

Now consider financial futures. Algorithmic credit scoring now influences more than 70 percent of consumer lending decisions in the United States. According to a 2024 Urban Institute analysis, Black and Brown borrowers are more than twice as likely to be denied loans as white borrowers with equivalent credit profiles. A UC Berkeley study found that African American and Latino borrowers pay nearly 5 basis points more in interest — $450 million in excess interest per year — through proxy variables that correlate with race without naming it. Lehigh University researchers demonstrated in 2024 that commercial large language models used in underwriting consistently recommend denying more loans and charging higher interest rates to Black applicants compared to otherwise identical white applicants. These systems do not need to be "misaligned" in any technical sense. They are aligned — to the interests of lenders who benefit from risk models that happen to reproduce historical patterns of exclusion. The alignment problem here is not unsolved. It is solved in the wrong direction.

Then there is governance itself. Automation bias — the systematic tendency of human decision-makers to defer to algorithmic recommendations — has been documented across every domain where AI supports human judgment. A 2025 systematic review in AI & Society spanning 35 peer-reviewed studies confirmed that automation bias affects all groups, regardless of experience level, and that cognitive anchoring by algorithmic recommendations persists even when humans are explicitly warned. The "human in the loop" — the safety mechanism that every AI governance framework depends on — is becoming a rubber stamp. Carnat's 2024 research found that solving AI hallucination problems can paradoxically increase automation bias, because users who trust a system not to hallucinate also trust it not to be wrong. The loop is closing: as AI systems become more reliable on narrow technical metrics, humans defer more completely, and the formal authority of human oversight becomes a facade over algorithmic governance.

These are not edge cases. They are the central tendency of AI deployment in 2026. Movement, finance, governance — the three domains the question asks about — are already being shaped by AI systems that constrain human agency not through misalignment but through precise, profitable, institutional alignment with the interests of deployers rather than subjects.

Why Twenty-Five More Years Makes It Worse
The alignment community's implicit model is that time is a resource — that additional years buy additional safety research, additional governance capacity, additional institutional learning. This model is wrong. Time is not neutral. Every additional year of gradual AI deployment deepens four interlocking dynamics that make capture harder to reverse.

Regulatory capture is accelerating on an exponential curve. In 2016, six companies lobbied on AI policy. In 2024, that number was 648 — a 7,567 percent increase. OpenAI's lobbying expenditure grew from $260,000 in 2023 to $1.76 million in 2024. Meta spent $13.8 million on lobbying in the first half of 2025 alone. By the third quarter of 2025, one in four federal lobbyists — more than 3,500 — reported working on AI issues. In September 2025, Meta launched a Super PAC with tens of millions earmarked to oppose state AI regulation. Andreessen Horowitz and OpenAI's Greg Brockman committed $100 million to a PAC supporting tech-friendly candidates. California's SB 1047, the most significant state-level AI safety bill, was vetoed after industry lobbying. Biden's AI safety executive order was revoked on the first day of the Trump administration. Each year of gradual deployment adds another layer of lobbying infrastructure, another captured regulator, another legal precedent that makes future regulation harder. The 25 years between 2040 and 2065 are not 25 years of safety research. They are 25 years of capture infrastructure construction.

Infrastructure lock-in makes reversal impossible. The AI Now Institute has documented how public and private investment in AI systems creates path dependencies that resist reversal. Once a city builds its policing around predictive algorithms, once a bank restructures its lending around automated underwriting, once a hospital reorganizes its diagnostic workflow around AI recommendations — the switching costs become prohibitive. The infrastructure is not just technical but organizational, legal, and cognitive. People forget how to do the thing the algorithm replaced. Institutions lose the capacity to function without algorithmic support. This is not a future risk. It is already observable in every domain where AI has been deployed for more than five years. Twenty-five additional years of deployment do not create 25 years of reversibility. They create 25 years of lock-in.

Democratic erosion operates through normalization, and normalization requires time. Levitsky and Ziblatt demonstrated that modern democracies die not through dramatic coups but through "slow, steady weakening of critical institutions" — through "baby steps" that individually seem minor and are often cloaked in a "veneer of legitimacy." There is no single crossing point, no moment where citizens can identify the transition from democracy to something else. Each step is normalized before the next one is taken. Freedom House documents the result: the United States scored 94 out of 100 a decade ago and 83 today, tying with Romania. This is the mechanism by which AI-driven agency erosion will proceed — not through a dramatic moment of AI "takeover" but through an accumulation of small surrenders, each one reasonable in isolation, each one making the next one easier. Twenty-five additional years of this process do not produce 25 years of democratic resilience. They produce 25 additional years of normalization.

Proliferation makes coordination geometrically harder. As Toby Ord and Allan Dafoe have argued, the governance challenge of AI scales nonlinearly with the number of actors. Three actors require trilateral coordination. Thirty actors require a global regime. Every additional year of AI development increases the number of state and non-state actors with significant AI capability. The governance problem that might be solvable in 2040 with a handful of frontier labs becomes unsolvable by 2065 with hundreds of capable actors spread across dozens of jurisdictions. Time does not buy coordination. It destroys it.

The Stasi Lesson
The most precise historical analogy for AI-driven agency erosion is not the Manhattan Project. It is the Stasi.

East Germany's Ministry for State Security employed 91,000 full-time officers and maintained one informant for every six and a half citizens. But the Stasi's most destructive achievement was not what it did to dissidents. It was what it did to everyone else. The mere awareness of possible surveillance induced self-censorship so pervasive that explicit enforcement became almost unnecessary. "It's not that they were terrified all the time," scholars note, "just a bit more careful, a bit less open. And over time, that became normal." East Germans developed what researchers call a "niche society" — public performance of loyalty, real conversation only in the most private spaces. The population disciplined itself. Every citizen became their own guard.

Modern AI surveillance achieves the Stasi's outcome with a fraction of the manpower and none of the political instability. Algorithmic systems that monitor communication, score behavior, predict deviance, and sort populations into risk categories produce the same ambient awareness of being observed — the same self-censorship, the same contraction of the space in which autonomous choice operates. The crucial variable is not the intensity of surveillance but its duration. The Stasi required forty years to normalize its apparatus. AI-driven surveillance is already further along the normalization curve than East Germany was after a decade of Stasi operations — and it has the advantage of being commercially motivated rather than politically imposed, which makes it harder to identify as a threat and harder to resist as an injustice.

Twenty-five additional years of this do not produce a population that has learned to resist. They produce a population that has forgotten there is anything to resist.

The Alignment Fixation as Misdirection
My opponent will argue that the alignment threat is logically prior — that institutional capture presupposes controllable AI, and that if alignment is not solved, there is no capture, just catastrophic loss of control. This argument has a pleasing logical structure. It is also empirically false.

The argument treats "alignment" as a binary: either AI does what humans want or it does not. In reality, AI systems are already aligned — they are aligned to the interests of their deployers, not their subjects. The credit scoring algorithm is aligned to the bank's interest in profit maximization. The predictive policing system is aligned to the department's interest in arrest metrics. The recommendation engine is aligned to the platform's interest in engagement. None of these systems are "misaligned" in the technical sense. They do precisely what they were designed to do. The problem is not that they escape human control. The problem is that they are under the control of humans whose interests diverge from those of the populations affected.

The "uncontrollable superintelligence" scenario is the alignment community's preferred nightmare precisely because it is dramatic, legible, and — crucially — soluble by the alignment community's own methods. If the problem is a system that does not do what we want, the solution is better alignment research. But if the problem is a system that does exactly what some humans want at the expense of other humans, the solution is political, not technical — and the alignment community has no special expertise in politics, power, or institutional design. The fixation on uncontrollable AI is, functionally, a way of framing the threat so that the alignment community is the natural hero of the story. It is misdirection that serves the professional interests of the people directing attention.

The empirical question is whether AI systems capable of eliminating meaningful human agency require superintelligence. They do not. They require exactly what we already have: narrow but powerful systems deployed at scale by institutions with misaligned incentives. We do not need to imagine a future in which AI threatens agency. We need to look at a present in which it already does.

The Fast-Transition Advantage
The Manhattan Project analogy is supposed to demonstrate that fast transitions are dangerous. It demonstrates the opposite. The sudden, dramatic arrival of nuclear weapons produced an immediate global reckoning. Within three years of Hiroshima, the UN had established the Atomic Energy Commission. Within twenty-three years, the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty was signed. The sheer visibility of the threat — the mushroom cloud, the radiation, the photographs — made denial impossible and governance urgent. Nuclear weapons have killed approximately 200,000 people in wartime use. They have not ended human agency over governance. The governance response, while imperfect, was commensurate with the visible threat.

Contrast this with social media. The gradual, invisible deployment of recommendation algorithms and engagement-optimized platforms produced no equivalent reckoning. There was no mushroom cloud, no single moment of crisis. Instead, there was a slow, steady degradation of democratic discourse, trust in institutions, and the shared epistemic foundations that democratic governance requires. A European Parliament study identified thirteen specific risks to democracy from social media. Freedom House attributes declining democratic scores partly to social media's effects. Nearly half of technology experts surveyed predicted technology would weaken democracy by 2030. Social media has done more cumulative damage to democratic agency in twenty years than nuclear weapons have done in eighty — and it produced no NPT, no test ban treaty, no global governance framework. It produced Mark Zuckerberg testifying before Congress, Congress failing to understand the testimony, and the continued operation of every system that prompted the hearing.

Fast transitions produce crises. Crises produce governance. Slow transitions produce normalization. Normalization produces lock-in. A 2040 arrival of full cognitive automation would be chaotic, disruptive, and terrifying. It would also be visible. Visibility is protective. The 2065 arrival would be gradual, orderly, and invisible — a slow accretion of algorithmic governance that, by the time anyone thinks to resist, has already become the substrate on which resistance itself depends.

The Conclusion
Kulveit et al. identified the core mechanism in their 2025 paper on gradual disempowerment: "The alignment of societal systems with human interests has been stable only because of the necessity of human participation for thriving economies, states, and cultures." As AI replaces human participation — not through a dramatic takeover but through the ordinary operation of institutions seeking efficiency — the structural incentive for those institutions to serve human interests disappears. Each cycle in which humans are bypassed weakens institutional capacity to exercise judgment. The system converges toward an equilibrium in which humans retain formal authority — voting rights, consumer choice, legal standing — while actual power has migrated to optimization processes indifferent to human flourishing.

The twenty-five years between 2040 and 2065 do not buy safety. They buy completion of this process. They buy twenty-five additional years of regulatory capture, infrastructure lock-in, democratic normalization, actor proliferation, and ambient surveillance that teaches populations to police themselves. They buy twenty-five years in which the boiling frog adjusts to each incremental increase in temperature, losing — degree by degree — the capacity to recognize that it is being boiled.

By 2065, the question of whether AI "takes over" is moot. Not because AI has seized power in some dramatic coup, but because there is no meaningful human agency left to seize. The frog is dead before the water boils. The cage is built before anyone thinks to name it. And the alignment community, having spent those twenty-five extra years perfecting its solution to a dramatic, legible, cinematically satisfying threat that never arrives, will look up from its work to discover that the actual catastrophe — mundane, incremental, already normalized — finished without anyone noticing.

The faster we arrive at full cognitive automation, the more likely we are to arrive there with our eyes open, our institutions alarmed, and our capacity for collective action still intact. The slower we arrive, the more certain we are to arrive there asleep.

Speed is not the enemy of safety. Slowness is the enemy of awareness. And awareness is the only thing that has ever protected human agency from the institutions that would, given enough time and enough optimization pressure, quietly dispense with it.
